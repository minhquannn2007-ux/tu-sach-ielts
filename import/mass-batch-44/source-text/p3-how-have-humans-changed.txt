At the University of Zurich, anatomist Frank Rühli is studying the remains of 2000 individuals, to help him discover how the human body has changed over the last few thousand years, thanks to the way society has developed and progressed. Some bones in his collection show the ravages of diseases that have long since been eradicated. The modern way of life, however, continues to transform not only our waistline but also our height, muscles and hormones.

Some transformations could be genetic in origin, but there is reason to suspect others are temporary ones that would melt away if we returned to a Stone Age environment. The sheer scale of the changes shows the ease with which the human body can adapt to new habitats over short timescales.

Discovering how those adaptations make us more vulnerable to certain diseases is an important facet of evolutionary medicine, an emerging specialty that views health through the lens of evolutionary theory. This approach sheds light on many common ailments that have arisen.

The idea that evolution could have been taking place in the past few thousand years goes against the traditional view. Weren’t we taught that natural selection operates over millions of years? Yet recent evidence indicates otherwise. A gene that enables people to digest milk after infancy, for example, was recently shown to have arisen with the invention of dairy farming several thousand years ago.

The genetic evidence stems from samples taken from people alive today. By looking at how a gene’s sequence varies among populations, we can work out how long ago it arose, and chart its global spread. But arguably, information about the past can be obtained more directly from preserved ancient human remains. By comparing them with modern-day humans, we can establish what civilisation has been doing to our bodies.

Perhaps the best-known difference clearly observed in the last 20 years is that westerners have got fatter, thanks to our calorie-rich diet and less active lifestyle. Obviously, that change would be reversed if we returned to hunting our food. Another trend is that we have been becoming less muscular. Bones that no longer support large muscles can themselves become weaker, so our shrinking musculature can be tracked in the fossil record. Our bones have become ‘gracile’, meaning the overall diameter has shrunk and the dense outer cortex has thinned. Christopher Ruff, of Johns Hopkins University in the USA, has x-rayed about 100 fossil leg bones, and examined bones dating back to 3 million years ago. Ruff’s team documented an average fall in bone strength of 15 per cent between 2 million and 5000 years ago. At that point, however, the trend accelerated, with another 15 per cent reduction over a mere 4000 years. Ruff thinks gracilisation kicked in when we began to use equipment that reduced physical exertion, starting with hand axes. How much of this process is due to genetic changes? It’s impossible to say, admits Ruff, but we know the body has an impressive capacity to respond to exertion over a single lifetime. Take professional tennis players: by looking at x-rays, Ruff’s team has worked out that the humerus in their playing arm is more than 40 per cent stronger than the corresponding bone in the opposite arm. For comparison, non-athletes have only a 5 to 10 per cent difference. This finding suggests that we retain our ancient capacity for strength. In turn, stronger bones mean fewer fractures. Broken hips, for example, were less common in the past and are rare in archaeological specimens, even accounting for the fact that lives were shorter.

There are other physical changes. Some of us seem to have acquired a new blood vessel in our arms, called the median artery. In fact, this blood vessel is present in the embryo but it normally vanishes around the eighth week of pregnancy, to be replaced by the ulnar and radial arteries. An increasing number of adults now retain this artery, up from 10 per cent at the beginning of the 20th century to 30 per cent at the end. Rühli’s former teacher, Maciej Henneberg, is helping document this change. It could be due to differences in the diet of pregnant mothers, he speculates, or modern medicine.

It is this kind of uncertainty that leaves some doctors unimpressed with evolutionary medicine. We cannot know if evolutionary explanations are true, since we rarely have a complete picture of the past, points out American family physician Harriet Hall. ‘Conventional medicine has a long record of successes,’ she says. ‘Evolutionary medicine hasn’t proven it has any real value.’ That’s not to say that documenting trends over time isn’t useful; if the arteries of a 20-year-old differ from those of a 90-year-old, it could affect their medical treatment. At the very least, surgeons must know how to operate safely.

Even our fingerprints have been the subject of research. Henneberg’s team took prints from 115 bodies, dividing them into two groups: those born before 1920 and those later. There were significant pattern differences. This may seem trivial to some, but one of Rühli’s next projects may shed light on the origin of serious disease. It is well known that in countries that suffer from malaria, a number of genetic changes have occurred. The point about these changes is that although they help protect people from malaria, they also lead to a higher risk of developing anaemia, a serious disease which affects the blood. By studying ancient bodies, Rühli’s team could narrow down the window in time when the genetic alterations arose.
