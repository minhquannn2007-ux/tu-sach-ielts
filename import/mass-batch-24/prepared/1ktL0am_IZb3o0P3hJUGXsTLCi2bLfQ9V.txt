Today, fire is used as a vital management tool for preserving both longleaf and its wildlife.
By deliberately causing what they call ‘prescribed burns’, conservationists can reduce the risk
of catastrophic conflagrations. ‘Forests are going to burn,’ says Amadou Diop, the National
Wildlife Federation’s (NWF) southern forests restoration manager. “It’s just a question of
when. With these low-intensity fires, we can pick the time and the place.’
Diop is spearheading a new NWF effort to restore longleaf. “It’s a species we need to go back
to,” he says. Educating landowners about the advantages of growing longleaf is a key part of
the program. ‘Right now, most existing longleaf is on public land,’ says Jerry MeCollum,
president of the Georgia Wildlife Federation. ‘Private land is where we need to work,” he _ ;
adds. pointing out that more than 90 percent of the area within the historic range of longleaf
falls under this category.
Interest among private landowners is growing throughout the South, but restoring longleaf is
not an casy task. It is not just a matter ‘of growing the trees. The herbaceous layer — the
understory of wiregrasses and other plants — also needs to be re-created. In areas where the
land has not been chewed up by farming, but converted to plantations of other pine species,
the seed bank of the longleaf forest usually remains viable beneath the soil, Where agriculture
has destroyed the seeds, however, wiregrass must be replanted. Right now, the expense is
prohibitive, but researchers are searching for low-cost solutions.
Bringing back longleaf is not for the short-sighted, however. Not many of us will be alive
when the pines being planted today become mature forests in 70 to 80 years. But that is not
stopping longleaf enthusiasts. ‘Today, it’s getting hard to find longleaf seedlings to buy,” says
one Louisiana landowner. ‘Everyone wants them. Longleaf is in a resurgence.”
