Using video diaries to collect research data from
children
Gaining knowledge of children’s experiences in relation to various life situations is an
essential task for researchers. However, using children as informants places large demands on
the researcher with regard to the method of data collection. In a project focusing on obtaining
knowledge of how children coped with illness involving other family members, we supplied
children with a video camera and gave them instruction in its use. We then gave the children
the task of carrying out daily video diary recordings at home over one month, in which they
recorded their feelings relating to the situation and their experiences over the day. It was
hoped that the information gathered would help us to achieve the best possible care for the
affected families.
We feel that the video diary method is particularly well suited for child research. In the
traditional research interview, where the child is face-to-face with an adult interviewer, the
uneven balance of power between the adult researcher and the child informant may affect the
nature of what the child says in the interview. Much of children’s lives are regulated by
adults, as illustrated for example by the requirement for them to follow school rules or do
their homework before playing computer games, and children may feel the need to provide
only the information they think the interviewer wants to hear. In addition, children may feel
under pressure in interviews because their language skills are limited, and the amount of
information gained during the interview may be relatively small, as their capacity for
concentration is often less than that of adults.
Compared with more traditional research methods, video diaries have several advantages.
They offer the opportunity to collect information on informants’ thoughts, feelings and
actions over a much longer period than other methods of collecting research data. In addition.
because the children and adolescents in our project had grown up with reality television
shows, they were more likely to feel confident about sharing their personal narratives in this
medium. We found the children immediately felt on home ground when the camcorders were
handed out to them, and many of them proved skilled in their use. For example, one evening
twelve-year-old Michael was sitting in front of the camera saying that he had had a good day
because the decoration of his new room had been completed. It had been painted in a super-
cool color, he told the camera, supporting his observation with a shot in which the camera
slowly panned around all four walls of his room before he continued his narrative. Indeed, for
some informants the camera might gradually become a sympathetic, sensitive and loyal friend
that they can turn to for a talk about their thoughts and feelings. This personification of the
video recorder was evident in our project, especially in the case of sixteen-year-old Peter. He
would start the daily recording by saying, ‘Today I’ve got something to tell you’ or ‘It is
probably going to be a bit boring today, because | have no news to tell you.’
In our project, we also benefited greatly from the opportunity to examine nonverbal
expressions among the children, testimonies that were sometimes at odds with what they

actually said. For example, on one occasion Michael told the camera about a day he had spent
playing cards in his grandmother’s house after he had been in trouble at home. Although his
description of the day was positive, it was also clear to us from his body language that he was
rather uneasy about the situation. Finally, the video offers the advantage of allowing the
repeated replaying of sections of the recordings in order to scrutinize details and correct
possible misconceptions.
However, the use of video diaries also has some limitations. One problem stems from the
informants’ freedom from the supervision exercised in a face-to-face interview. Although the
children were encouraged to use the camera at the same hour every day, the researchers were
not present and had no way to ensure that they actually did so. Ifa child did not feel like
recording a certain day, they could choose not to. The most significant limitation, however, is
the lack of opportunity for the researchers to investigate in detail the information given by the
informants while the recording is being made.
To address this problem, in this project we scheduled an interview with the informants after
we had reviewed and analyzed the videotapes so that any unresolved issues could be settled.
In this follow-up interview we also asked the children about their experiences of using the
camera. Michael said that for him it was peculiar to talk to the camera in the beginning, but he
got used to it and found the possibility of describing his thoughts and feelings very liberating.
Another child, Christian, had found it very difficult to talk about his feelings, especially his
very pronounced angry emotions, but the video diary gave him an opportunity to reflect
further on this, and he subsequently indicated that it had helped him to overcome the problem.
