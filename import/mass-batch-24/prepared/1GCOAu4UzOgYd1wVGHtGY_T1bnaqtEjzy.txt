To Knowles, everyone was a critic, but
constructively so. Even Meyer concluded
his letter with a positive prediction, “The
finished product will stand for more years
than you and I will be around; we felt it was
important enough to make any suggestions
that might improve it.’
After nine months of fine-tuning,
Knowles felt the portrait was finished. The
AGA flew the artist and his painting from
Maine to Toronto, where the group’s
annual meeting was held. That night, his
first ever in a hotel, Knowles was called
upon one more time to make an adjustment
to the painting. The committee had decided
to ‘dehorn’ its ideal cow: the first dairy
breed to do so. The ever-patient Knowles
obliged by painting out the horns; an
adjustment that can be seen on the original
painting today.
The next day must have been a relief for
the young farmer. While he ended up living
a long and happy life — becoming a
Guernsey breeder of renown — he probably
never again reached the heights of that
moment when the curtain was raised and
his painting revealed. However, today his
painting continues to epitomise an ideal
Guersey, and his moment of perfection is
still on display for all to see.
