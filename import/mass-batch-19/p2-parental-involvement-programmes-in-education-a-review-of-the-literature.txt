Parental involvement programmes in education:
a review of the literature

Attempts to enhance parental involvement in education occupy governments,
administrators, educators and parents' organisations across North America, Australasia,
continental Europe, Scandinavia and the UK. It is widely felt that parents should play a role
not only in the promotion of their own children's achievements, but more broadly in
school improvement and the democratisation of school governance. The European
Commission, for example, holds that the degree of parental participation is a significant
indicator of the quality of schooling.

Parental participation might take place through:

•  statutory advisory and decision-making bodies
•  evaluation of their schools
•  voluntary associations
•  voluntary involvement in after-school activities
•  voluntary involvement in classroom activities
•  communications with the school

The business of enhancing parental participation is widespread, lively and intensely active.
Entering the phrase 'parents in education' or its synonyms into any search engine produces
an indigestible response. One online search engine, for example, locates in excess of three
million sites for the term 'parents in education'. From the point of view of this report, the
question is not so much 'what is going on?' but 'what works?' and 'what lessons are being
learned?'.

McKenna and Williams (1998) published an extensive review of 'What works in Canada'
with regard to home-school co-operation. The authors were able to report the development
of policies at the level of parental involvement in school governance. They also present
evidence on the rapid growth of parental engagement in this sphere: developments in
information technology have increased the capacity of parents and schools to exchange
messages with each other. There is also an extensive and well-documented level of
parental voluntary work in schools. However, the writers also reported that, although the
importance of schools helping parents to enhance home learning environments is widely
recognised in Canada, 'evidence of action in this area is unfortunately sparse'. They also
recognise that, in general, 'movement towards greater parental participation has proceeded
without strong legislation', and that the approach to running the programmes could be
described as haphazard and disorganised. McKenna and Williams' review is data-free in
terms of impact on pupils' achievement and adjustment.

In the United States of America, attempts to enhance parental involvement have been
much more systematic than is evident in Canada. Parental involvement programmes have
featured in federal, state and local education policies (Epstein, 1991). Parent involvement is
one of the six targeted areas in the 'No Child Left Behind Act' of 2001. Schools receiving
maximum federal funding are required to spend part of the money on parent participation
programmes. However, reviews of these programmes show that few are independently
evaluated (Henderson, 1987). Moreover, where sound evaluations have been conducted,
there seems to be little evidence that the programmes are effective in improving student
achievement or in changing the behaviour of parents, pupils or teachers. White, Taylor and
Moss (1992) analysed 172 studies of the impact of parental involvement programmes at
pre-school level in the US, and concluded that there was 'no convincing evidence that the
ways in which parents have been involved result in more effective outcomes'.

One study which did claim positive effects for parental involvement concerned families
with low socio-economic status (Armor, 1976). The study found that parents in this group
were often under-represented among the ranks of those involved with schools. There were
numerous reasons for this: lack of time or energy due to long hours of heavy physical work,
embarrassment about their own educational level, lack of understanding of information
about the structure of the school and accepted communication channels, perceived lack of
welcome by teachers and administrators, and teachers' and administrators' assumptions of
parents' disinterest or inability to help with children's schooling. However, the researchers
found that parents of disadvantaged children did make a positive contribution to their
children's achievement in school, regardless of their own standard of education, provided
that they received adequate training and encouragement.

Nevertheless, the main problem confronting reviewers in the field is the poor quality of
evaluations. There is a huge level of industry in the field: Mattingly (2002) found hundreds
of reports of parent involvement programmes. However, amongst those which included
information about the outcomes of interventions there were 'glaring flaws', including a
failure to report crucial information on processes and participants, lack of comparison
groups to account for maturation effects, a reliance on highly subjective indicators of
effectiveness, and a lack of control of the effects of socio-economic status.

In the light of all the above, we can only conclude that there is no substantial evidence to
indicate a causal relationship between interventions designed to increase parent
involvement and improvements in student learning. This statement does not, of course,
imply that the programmes were ineffective. Rather, it cautions that the evidence for their
success does not justify the widespread assumptions currently made about parental
involvement.
