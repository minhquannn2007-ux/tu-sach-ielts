              Science and knowledge

 An examination of the power and the limitations of science through history

If one were to compare the images seen by an 11th-century philosopher and a present-
day physicist looking out over the countryside  in any European country, there would be
little to differentiate them – power lines and distant motorways apart. But the differences
in how they made sense  of what they saw would be  vast. The meaning  of the world
stretching  out before them would depend upon  the conceptual scheme each used as
the   filter,  organiser  and  interpreter  of   their  experience.  Both  schemes  would  be
beautiful,  rich  and  immensely  complex;  both  would  be  in many  ways  strange  and
powerful.  But  the scheme  of  the  present-day  physicist  is  incomparably  stranger and
more  powerful,  because whereas  the  source  of  the  11th-century  philosopher's  vision
would  have  been  largely  imaginative  and  subjective,  the  source  of  the  modern
physicist's vision is intellectual and objective.

Over   the   centuries,   science  has   transformed   mankind's   self-understanding  and
relationship  to the world. The popular science books  that now abound permit everyone
to become  familiar with advances  in  biological science, fundamental physics, and other
fields  that  attract  public  attention.  This  is  positive;  the more  scientifically  literate  the
world becomes  the  better. However,  today  scientific  illiteracy  is  widespread,  and  the
major reason  for  that  is  usually  attributed  to  science's sheer  complexity.  Non-scientists
find   it  difficult  to comprehend, and the consequent ignorance  is the source  of  mistrust
and  fear. To many, science now seems  to promise  frightening, unknown consequences
of  genetic  engineering,  pollutions  of  various  kinds, and  other  unforeseeable  dangers.
An  extraordinary  anti-scientific  coalition  of people has  arisen,  consisting  of some who
are ignorant of science, and others who correctly understand only too well the bad uses that
it can be turned to.

Defenders  of  science  argue  that  the  intention  of  science  is  not  to  threaten human
interests,  to pose environmental danger  or  to view  the  world  negatively.  Instead they
affirm  the  progressive  and  liberating  aspects  of  science,  and  say  that    if  these
were  better  understood  there  would  be  no  damaging  divide  between  what  the
philosopher C P Snow  labelled the  'two  cultures'  (that  is, the  cultures  of science and
of  the  humanities).  Proponents  of  science  are  particularly  concerned  to  show  that
most  fears  expressed  by  science's  enemies  are  misplaced,  arguing  that  science
actually  provides  the remedy  for  the  difficulties   it sometimes  creates, and  that   it  will
continue to generate benefits as it progresses, just as it has in the past.

No   doubt   this  response    is   largely   right.  Most   applications   of   science   are
beneficial: consider electric lights, medical technology, microchips, air travel, and much else.
But  scientist and  non-scientist  alike  must  acknowledge  that  there  can be  problems,
such as the dangers caused by radioactive fallout from nuclear accidents, and the disasters
that might accrue from mistakes in genetic manipulation. One can be a strong supporter
of science without covering up  risk or making unconvincing excuses. And one can  still

say that  little in human history compares with the magnificence of science, or the power
and truth of the scientific method.

Under  the  accepted  rules  of  that  method,  a  researcher  proceeds  by  subjecting  a
hypothesis  to  rigorous  examination,  wherever  possible  experimentally  in  an  effort  to
prove the hypothesis  false.  If the hypothesis does not  turn out  to be wrong, then the
theory stands for the time being. However, failure to prove a theory false does not mean
the  theory  is  actually  true,  only  that   it can be used  until something  better  appears.
Accordingly   all  science  must be  continually  defended;  further  and  stronger  evidence
can always refute or change  it. The essence of science  is healthy scepticism and open-
mindedness.

Of  course,  science  does  have  its  limitations. Each  branch  of  science  is  concerned
with  a  defined  range  of  phenomena.  Botanists,  geologists  and  meteorologists  study
plants,  rocks and  weather  respectively. No  branch  tries  to  explain  the  achievements
of  the  Renaissance  Italian  artist  Michelangelo  (1475-1564)  or  the  ethical  dimension,
say,  of  political  choices. These  matters –  art and  politics –  are  subjects  for debate
themselves, and no  scientist would presume  that answers  to  the problems they pose
can  be  discovered  by  science.   Critics  of  science,  however,  have  a  vague  and
certainly mistaken notion that  this  latter  is what,  in  fact,  scientists aspire  to. This leads
some  critics  to accuse  scientists  of  getting involved  in issues where   it  is inappropriate
for them to do so, and others  to complain when science  fails  to address such issues.
Both criticisms reflect a hopeless ignorance of what is being attacked.

All things considered, the achievements of science have been truly heroic. Time and again,
it has  forced us  to  reconsider what are thought  to be  basic  truths, and  to  learn  to
overcome  the  intellectual  challenges  that  that  reconsideration  poses.  In  the  early
20th  century,  the  Austrian  physicist  Ludwig  Boltzmann  proclaimed  that  the  new
and  then  oftentimes dangerous  field  of  aviation needed  genius-heroes. He was  right:
it took heroism to risk one's  life in the fragile aircraft that first ventured into the skies, and
it  required  genius  to  discover  and  apply  the  rules  of  aerodynamics  to  building
those  strange-looking  flying  machines.  Within  a  few  years  ordinary  people became
aviators, who for their own self-interest in the air learned things like the angle of glide as
well as any  physicist. But  scientists today continue  to be heroes and geniuses. They
are most often quiet heroes and unknown geniuses, but  their accomplishments continue
to be breathtaking.
