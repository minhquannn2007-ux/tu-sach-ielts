  A Digital Cinema of the Mind?

  Should we be concerned about the effects of digitization* on the cinema?

A   In a few years, film will become a historical curiosity. And the three symbols for the
    motion picture – the reel, the clapstick and film itself, with those little square
    sprocket holes running along its edges – may be all that is left of the technical
     skills and knowledge of twentieth-century film-making.

B   But is the digitization of cinema something that will ultimately be good for it? One
   way to investigate this question is to examine the transformation in painting that
    took place in the 15th century, when the old technique of fresco, the painstaking
    process whereby damp plaster is applied to a wall and then stained with pigments
    that bond chemically with the plaster, was largely replaced by oil paint on canvas.

C  Some of the greatest triumphs of European pictorial art were done in fresco. A
    great deal of planning needed to be done when working in this medium, and the
     variables – like the consistency and drying time of the plaster – had to be controlled
     exactly. Artists needed a precise knowledge of the pigments and how they would
    change color as they dried. Once the pigment had been applied, no revisions were
     possible. For all these reasons, fresco painting was an expensive effort of many
    people and various interlocking technologies, overseen by the artist who took
     responsibility for the final product.

D  The invention of oil paint changed all this. The artist was freed to paint wherever
    and whenever he wanted. He did not have to create the work in its final location.
    The paint was the same color wet as it would be dry. And the artist could paint
    over areas he didn't like, even to the point of re-using canvases for completely
     different purposes. Although painting in oils remained collaborative for a while, the
   new medium allowed the artist more and more control of every aspect of the work,
     intensifying his personal vision. This was tremendously liberating, and the history
     of art from 1450 to the present is a clear testimony to the creative power of that
     liberation -and some of its risks, which found their ultimate expression in the 19th
    and 20th centuries, with the emergence of solitary and tortured geniuses like the
    Dutch painter Van Gogh.

E   Working with conventional film has been more like making a fresco than an oil
     painting. It is so heterogeneous, with so many technologies woven together in a
    complex and expensive fabric, that it is almost by definition impossible for a single
    person to control. By contrast, since digital techniques are all based on the same

    mathematical procedures and naturally tend to integrate with each other, they
    come under the control of a single person more easily.

F   So let's suppose a technical high point is reached some time in the middle of the
    21st century, when it somehow becomes possible for one person to make an entire
     feature film digitally, with virtual actors. Would this be a good thing? If the history
     of oil painting is any guide, the broadest answer would be yes, with the obvious
    caution to keep a wary eye on the destabilizing effect of following too intently an
     overly personal vision. One need only look at some of the more bizarre and
     idiosyncratic paintings or classical music compositions of the 20th century to see
    the potential perils of this.

G   Let's go even further, and force the issue to its ultimate conclusion by supposing
    the invention of a device that could directly convert a single person's thoughts into
    a viewable cinematic reality. You would attach a series of electrodes to various
     points on your skull and simply think the film into existence. The kinds of film-
    makers who would accept, even leap at, this possibility are driven by the desire to
    see their own vision on screen in as pure a form as possible. The director Alfred
    Hitchcock would probably have been one of them, judging from his description of
    the creative process: 'The film is already made in my head before we start shooting.'
    Those who would be more likely to reject it are the film-makers who are interested
    above all in the collaborative process of film-making and in seeing a detailed vision
    mysteriously emerge out of that process. The director Francis Ford Coppola's
     colorful description of his role sums it up: 'The director is the ringmaster of a circus
    that is inventing itself.'

H  The paradox of cinema is that it is most effective when it fuses two contradictory
    elements-the general and the personal – into a kind of mass intimacy. The work
      itself is aimed at an audience of millions, and yet, at its best, it seems to speak to
    each member of the audience personally. And collaboration may be the very thing
    that allows this to happen. Every person who works on a film brings a particular
     perspective to bear on it, and if these perspectives are properly orchestrated by the
     director, the result is a multi-faceted and yet integrated complexity that will catch
    and sustain the interest of the audience. There will be collaboration in motion
     pictures, grudging or not, for many years to come. But it seems that if we are
    looking out for the shadow that digital might cast, we might look in the direction of
    anything that encourages a solitary, monolithic vision and discourages developed
    complexity, both at the beginning, in the process of filming, and at the end, in the
     theatrical enjoyment.
