            Back to their roots

   For her book, Naming of Names, Anna Pavord travelled extensively to
                    investigate the origins of plant names

After the fall of Constantinople in 1453, when European embassies were established in
the capital of the new Ottoman empire, the way was clear for Eastern plants to be
introduced into Europe. And they were, in increasingly large numbers. In the hundred
years between the mid-15th and mid-16th centuries, 20 times as many plants entered
Europe along the long-established Silk Road as had arrived in the previous 2,000
years.

Since medieval times, in Western Europe Latin had been the language of the written
word, understood equally well in France, Italy, England or the Netherlands. The
practice of applying Latin names to plants in the first books about herbs was gradually
perfected during the next 300 years until it became a special botanical language, a
kind of Latin Esperanto, understood by anyone, anywhere in the world, who is
interested in plants.

Many plants were brought into Europe for medicine, to increase the range of drugs
available to apothecaries*. At the time of the first books on herbs, most medicines
came from these plants (‘simples’ they were called) and new ingredients gave hope of
new cures, provided the ingredients were true to name. A plant’s pharmaceutical value
depended on the plant-hunter’s ability to distinguish one species from another; its
economic value would increase in equal measure.

But apothecaries worried that they were often tricked with substitutes, plants that
were more easily obtained than the real thing. This is why, on 13 July 1629, the young
English apothecary Thomas Johnson set out with a group of like-minded friends on an
expedition to Kent in the southeast of England. It was the first of a series of journeys
they planned into different areas of the country, collecting the wild plants they saw,
noting their characteristics and uses and trying, for the first time, to establish some
kind of consensus as to what plants grew in Britain and what they should be called.
This identification and naming of names was a process that had been going on in Italy
and France for some time before it happened in England; Johnson’s journey and the
motive behind it had been inspired by the young Italian botanist Ulysse Aldrovandi’s
expedition to the Sibilline Mountains in northern Italy in 1557, the first journey ever
undertaken in Europe with the aim of recording the plants of a specific area.
Aldrovandi, of course, would not have called himself a botanist. That word didn’t
appear in print until the English plantsman, John Ray, introduced it at the beginning
of the 18th century.

The study of plants, though, was intimately connected with the study of medicine. A
16th-century apothecary, surgeon or doctor had to be a plantsman. Aldrovandi was
part of a pan-European network that connected all those interested in a better
understanding of the natural world. By introducing a system of nomenclature, they
hoped to bring order to that world, setting alongside the common names a set of Latin
names, agreed by gradual consensus and, advanced by contact with other enquirers in
other countries, a system that could be universally understood.

The earliest printed plant book, a German herbal, appeared within 30 years of
Gutenberg inventing the printing press in 1465, but the first bestseller, the first new
printed herbal to be read throughout Europe, was written in 1530 by Otto Brunfels, a
schoolmaster. The key to the book’s success was not the words, mostly cobbled
together from classical texts, but the illustrations contributed by Hans Weiditz,
draughtsman, engraver and pupil of the great Albrecht Dürer. Weiditz drew direct from
life and he created the first printed images of plants that could be unequivocally
recognised throughout Europe. Artists, not writers, paved the way for the botanical
Renaissance in Europe.

But with each new wave of introductions from abroad, the pressure to sort,
discriminate and organise plants into a rational system of nomenclature increased.
When plants began to pour in from the newly settled lands of America, the task
became even more urgent. The Spaniard Nicolas Monardes was the first to describe
the numerous hitherto unknown plants that grew in this strange territory and already
by 1577 his book, which included reports of novelties such as tobacco, had been
translated into English.

The narrative of the Naming of Names effectively ends with the death of John Ray at
the beginning of the 18th century. Isolated in his Essex cottage, Ray set down his last
words on the subject that had intrigued him since his student days in Cambridge. The
Six Rules that Ray proposed provided the vital underpinning of a new discipline, and
his insistence on the importance of method before system was critical in shaping
future thinking on the subject. With Ray’s death, the study of plants left the
philosophers behind and engaged instead with a new breed of men, the scientists of
the Enlightenment.

Of course, the story does not really end with Ray. He established the study of plants as
a scientific discipline and gave this study a new name: botany. His work helped to shift
the study of plants away from superstition and towards science, but this particular
story cannot have an end. As ways of seeing change, new things are seen, new
relationships are revealed. Ahead of Ray are electron microscopes, Watson and Crick,
the double helix, DNA. The task of defining and categorising the natural world, which
had previously belonged to philosophers and naturalists, has now, in the 21st century,
been taken over by physicists, phytochemists and molecular systematists.
