The problem with aiming for average
At 18 years old, Todd Rose was regarded as academically weak because his grades
were consistently low. He went on to do a series of minimum-wage jobs in factories,
retail stores and call centres. Rose says that no matter what he tried, everything ended
in disaster. Now, at 42, Rose is the director of the Mind, Brain and Education
programme at Harvard Graduate School of Education, which gives him a sense of
fulfilment and achievement. However, he admits that he often felt extremely worried and
stressed about his future. So what happened? ‘I'd like to say | was smart and figured it
out,’ he says. ‘In reality, it was desperation,’ Eventually, he stopped trying to follow the
path that everyone else was following and instead, he created a pathway that suited his
way of learning. As he climbed the career ladder, there was an element of luck involved,
too, when he met people who became mentors. But Rose believes the real key to his
success was a shift in thinking.
Rose has recently been asking people to question the assumptions we make, about
ourselves and others, that limit our potential. The enemy is what he calls
‘averagarianism’, The notion of the ‘average man’ emerged in the early 19th century
when the academic discipline of statistics was first developed. It was astrologers that
gave this new science its name, but then a Belgian mathematician, Adolphe Quetelet,
saw it might be useful in measuring the irregular patterns of complex social phenomena
such as crime or marriage rates. Quetelet calculated the average of various human
attributes: chest circumferences, weight, height and so on. To him, the average
represented perfection.
In the late 1800s, Frederick Winslow Taylor took Quetelet's views further. A pioneer of
management consultancy, his vision for greater efficiency in the industrialised US was
that businesses, rather than encouraging employees’ talents, should hire average men
who fitted the system. Taylor believed there was only one way to accomplish any
process: the standardised way, and his philosophy still powers businesses today. Rose
had personal experience of this ‘averagarianism’ while working in a call centre and an
aluminium factory. Here, he found he was a cog in a machine, a worker expected to
conform to the average, with no opportunity to show initiative or use his individual
talents.
As the world became increasingly standardised, ‘averagarian' ideas were embraced by
social policy makers, educators and politicians, and remain a powerful force, influencing
the design of institutions and how we measure human potential and performance. The
assumptions are that the average represents most of us, and the way we deviate from it
defines us; the ideal is to be better than average at everything. ‘I don't think most of us
are aware of the impact this has on our lives,’ says Rose. But averages are a constant
feature for the media: average house prices, average student debt, average earnings.
More critically, the way we are evaluated academically or ranked in the workplace is
based around how closely we approximate to the average or how far we can exceed it.
Rose doesn't accept this approach. He believes we should recognise that we all have
something unique to offer, and adapt our systems accordingly. He has a few ideas,
based on his experience of tapping into his full potential while at college. On discovering
that his academic counsellor at Weber State University had made much the same
course recommendations to another student with a very different background, Rose
decided to structure his study to suit his areas of interest, strengths and weaknesses.
He chose to take the most interesting courses in the first two years because he knew
they would hold his attention. He played to his own personal preferences by signing up
for classes involving discussion, rather than long lectures. By the time the duller study

No-one is average, according to science. For instance, when neuroscientist Michael
Miller did MRI scans of brains, he observed that no two shared the same pattern of
activity when memory was being tested. This goes against traditional thinking.
Psychologist Karen Adolph’s work has disproved the widely-held belief that we all follow
a similar learning path to walking in our early years. Averages don’t work for sport,
either. Rose notes that head coach Isiah Thomas selected basketball players for the
New York Knicks on the basis of the average number of points they scored per game:
then the team hit a four-year losing streak. Thomas had overlooked the fact that
basketball skills are multi-dimensional, and being good at scoring doesn't mean a player
will excel at all aspects of the game. The paradox is that strong teamwork requires
individuality, something Thomas came to learn and which Rose hopes more people will
also recognise.
