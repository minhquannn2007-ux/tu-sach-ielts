The development of the bicycle
In a recent survey of British radio listeners,
the bicycle was voted the most significant
technological invention since 1800. As with
many other technological innovations,
however, it is difficult to determine exactly
by whom or when the bicycle was invented.
In fact, what we recognise as a bicycle
today was never created fully formed, but
rather represents a process of evolution,
the product of incremental changes
initiated by many individuals over time.
The Italian artist and inventor Leonardo
da Vinci (1452-1519) is often cited as the
first person to visualise the concept of a
bicycle, but he never constructed one, and
there are doubts surrounding the
authenticity of a sketch of a two-wheeled
device discovered among his papers. In
about 1790, the French Comte de Sivrac
was the first person to build what could be
said to resemble a bicycle. This was a
wooden-framed device known as a
Celerifere. With no pedals or steering
mechanism, the Celerifere was considered
a child's toy rather than a method of adult
transport.
In 1817 Baron von Drais, a German
inventor, built a device with two wheels of
the same size mounted in line on a wooden
frame. The users sat on a seat on a frame
with a leg on either side of the frame and
simply pushed themselves along with their
feet. It was known as a Draisienne or
hobby horse, and although it could be
steered it proved impractical for
transportation anywhere but on a smooth
road or path.
The next appearance of a two-wheeled
device was in 1865, when pedals were
attached directly to the front wheel. This
device was officially called a velocipede,
but was popularly known as the bone
shaker as its wooden (later metal) wheels
made for an extremely uncomfortable ride.
However, riding velocipedes for pleasure
became popular, and indoor riding tracks
became fairly common in large cities as a
result.
The desire for speed was the driving
factor behind the next significant step in the
development of two-wheeled transport,
which took place in the 1870s. The most
obvious way to increase the speed that
could be obtained was to increase the size
of the wheel to which the pedals were
attached. The front wheels became larger
and larger as manufacturers realised that
the bigger the wheel, the further the rider
could travel with one rotation of the pedals.
At about the same time, the first all-metal
two-wheeled devices appeared due to
advances in the production of strong,
relatively light steel. Then solid rubber tyres
were added, providing a somewhat
smoother ride. These newly adapted
devices, called High Wheelers, or Penny
Farthings, were the first to actually be
referred to as bicycles. The rider sat on a
seat above the large front wheel, which
made them awkward to mount. They were
notoriously difficult to ride as they were
extremely unstable except when ridden at
speed.
Because of the problems riders
encountered with high wheelers, an
alternative device was specifically designed
for females. This version had smaller
wheels and incorporated a third wheel for
greater stability — one in the front and two
in the rear. This tricycle was considered
more practical for women, since their long
dresses were potentially hazardous when
riding High Wheelers.
The next important change in bicycle
design led to the creation in the 1880s of
the basic form of the modern bicycle. The
safety bicycle, as the new device was
known, saw a return to two same-size
wheels. It was powered by pedals in the
middle of the frame. These pedals were
attached with a chain and gears to the rear
wheel. This arrangement meant that the
rider, seated on the frame between the
wheels, could attain the same speed as
with the high wheeler, but without the
dangers associated with the huge front
wheel. In addition to these design changes,
there was the introduction of the pneumatic
tyre, developed in 1888 by Scottish
veterinary surgeon John Dunlop. This
involved a tube inflated with air being
inserted inside the hard rubber outer tyre,
thus providing a considerably more
pleasant ride.
Manufacturers experimented with
various designs, but the Rover, created by
English inventor and industrialist John
Starley, became the most successful and
most copied. Virtually all the Rover's
features are present on today's bicycles: a
diamond-shaped frame, pedals below the
saddle that power the back wheel through
a chain and gears, and handle bars
attached to the front wheel.
The bicycle became incredibly popular
in Europe and America in the 1890s, which
is often referred to as the Golden Age of
Cycling. The bicycle was a practical and
relatively inexpensive investment for the
working man. It was an efficient means of
transport and gave him much greater
flexibility in his leisure time. Women, once
forced by practical considerations to ride
heavy tricycles, could now ride a much
more versatile machine that increased their
mobility considerably.
Over the past 30 years or so, the world
has arguably seen the greatest changes to
the bicycle since the safety bicycle was
introduced over 100 years ago. The
availability of a wider range of materials,
improved manufacturing techniques, better
scientific knowledge and ever-increasing
popularity have led to the introduction of
many specialised bicycle models — from
commuter bikes and collapsible bikes to
mountain bikes and racing bikes. The
advantages of cycling, including its health
benefits, low costs and reduced
environmental impact, have all increased
the appeal of this remarkable invention.
