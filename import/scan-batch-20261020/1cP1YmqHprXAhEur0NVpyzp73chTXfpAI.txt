The Health Benefits of Singing
How music can serve as a treatment for some medical issues
Most of us are well aware of the emotional and restorative power of music. For Dermot
O'Callaghan, head of the Association of Irish Choirs, the social aspect is of key importance.
‘When you sing in a choir, you come together and go through the singing process
communally.’ O'Callaghan points out that many choir members enjoy the company, and adds
that singing in a choir is an increasingly common pastime. It is something that appeals
equally to all ages and enhances people's lives.
But there's more to it than that. Singing is beneficial to your vocal cords and improves health
overall, claims O'Callaghan. Time and time again, he says, singers will report that a
rehearsal or performance has energised them and given them an enormous feel-good factor.
This is because the entire body is involved in singing. He cites elevated mood, improved
recall of past events and increased concentration as some of the results of singing regularly.
He adds that stress and anxiety have also been proven to be significantly reduced after
singing. This could be because singing, like all forms of music, does not involve the
reasoning parts of the brain. These are the parts which cause us to over-think things, to fret
and worry.
Evidence from different research projects is also mounting for the therapeutic power of
singing, for its usefulness in the rehabilitation of broken or damaged brains. Researchers at
the Centre for Brain Research (CBR) at the University of Auckland are looking at this in
relation to the CeleBRation Choir, a group of men and women with neurological conditions
such as Parkinson's (a movement disorder caused by gradual loss of muscle control) and
aphasia (an acquired language disorder), who gather each week to sing their way through a
variety of traditional and modern songs.
The choir, led by music therapist Alison Talmage, is the first of its type to be set up in New
Zealand, but the concept is catching on. The CeleBRation choir was started up by CBR’s
communications manager, Laura Fogg, who was inspired by a similar choir established by a
hospital in London. ‘We just thought it would be jolly good fun, but then our members started
telling us that this was really helping them.’ The members have clearly been persuasive;
Fogg, a science graduate, has recently enrolled in a Masters degree, to research whether
the choir really is benefiting its members in a way that is distinctive from other uplifting group
activities such as board games or exercise classes.
First, there are some general observations we can make about the brain and singing. It’s not
uncommon for people who have suffered a brain injury to be unable to string a sentence
together but still be able to sing a favourite song. One member of the CeleBRation choir can
say only a few words, but reputedly performs an excellent solo of a traditional folk tune with
remarkable confidence. It seems one part of the brain is wired for speech, but several parts
are wired for music, so it’s possible music could be used to form new connections between
different parts of the brain. To put things more simply, people might be able to sing their way
back to speech. One research study has shown that when people with brain injuries sing
regularly, the quantity of their speech is affected and their language is also much clearer
compared to a control group that were not choir members.
This, however, is distinct from what people with Parkinson's disease might get out of singing.
Parkinson's is a motor-system disorder, and speaking is an extremely complex motor-
controlled activity - many people with Parkinson's may lose some volume from their voice as
well as the precision and intonation of their speech. No one is suggesting singing could
prevent that, but it may slow things down. ‘You have to put a lot of expression into your voice
when you're singing .,. so maybe that will have a therapeutic effect that will carry over into
conversation,’ says Suzanne Purdy, head of Speech Sciences at the University of Auckland.
Many members of the CeleBRation choir and their families are convinced that the singing is a
helpful form of therapy, reporting all sorts of physiological and psychological benefits. Fogg
and Purdy have also conducted a survey of the choir members, and results have shown that
the friendships they have made have also enhanced their lives. At the same time, one-on-
one speech therapy and one-on-one music therapy are widely regarded as effective, but few
people are fortunate enough to get such specialised care, and sometimes motivation to
continue long term with therapy is an issue. Patients can get bored and demoralised. Choral
singing, however, could be a form of treatment that the elderly living in residential homes
might benefit from, and as a form of therapy, singing is cheap in comparison to alternative
methods.
