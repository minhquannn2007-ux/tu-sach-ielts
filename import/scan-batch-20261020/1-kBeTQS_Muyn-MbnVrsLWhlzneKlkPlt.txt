The Great Tit
The Great Tit (Parus major) is a widespread
and common species of bird found in all
types of woodland throughout Europe, the
Middle East, Central and Northern Asia,
and part of North Africa. It is generally
resident in one place: most Great Tits do
not migrate except in extremely harsh
winters. Until recently this species was
grouped with numerous other subspecies.
However, DNA studies have now shown
these other subspecies to be distinct from
the Great Tit, and they have been classified
as two separate species: the Cinereous Tit
of Southern Asia, and the Japanese Tit of
East Asia. The Great Tit remains the most
widespread species in the genus Parus.
One of the first things to notice about
the Great Tit is its size: it is larger than
other tits. This fact may be related to its
foraging behaviour. The Great Tit is less
agile than the other tits, and spends more
time feeding from the ground, a behaviour
often seen in the autumn when there are
plenty of fallen seeds. Its larger size also
means that the Great Tit tends to be
dominant over the other species, often
evicting them from the tree holes and
spaces which it then uses as nesting
cavities.
Although male and female Great Tits
appear superficially similar, a difference
can be observed in the black stripe that
extends down the chest and belly. In male
birds this passes between the base of the
legs. In females the stripe is reduced in
width and does not reach the base of the
legs. In addition it often contains some pale
feathering, giving it a more washed-out
appearance. In males the main plumage
colours tend to be brighter and glossier
than they are in females. As is the case in
many other species, the plumage colours
tend to get stronger with age. Prior to their
autumn moult, young birds can be
recognised by their pale yellow, rather than
white, cheeks and by the different colour of
some of their wing feathers.
Great Tits usually nest in tree cavities,
and the female constructs the nest alone.
She begins by building a substantial base
of moss, with the addition of some grass
and other plant fibre. Regardless of the
floor space available within the cavity, she
will cover the entire area with the same
depth of material, so if the cavity is large
the task is considerable. A nest cup is then
added, lined with fur, wool, hair and other
soft materials (though rarely feathers), into
which the eggs will be deposited.
Once egg-laying begins, the female will
usually lay one egg on a daily basis,
though she may occasionally miss a day,
something which is more common in early
nesting attempts than in later ones. Egg-
laying takes place early in the morning,
after which the female covers the eggs with
nesting material and spends time with her
mate, before returning to the nest in the
evening to roost.

period between male and female. The male
is busy protecting his mate from other male
birds, and will often feed the female,

Incubation does not begin properly until
all the eggs have been laid, so the
presence of the female roosting overnight
on the eggs does not necessarily mean
that it has started. There is insufficient
warmth for the incubation to begin unless
the eggs are brought into contact with the
female's brood patch, a small section of
skin well-supplied with blood vessels that
develops on her belly for that purpose.
Each Great Tit chick weighs roughly 1.5
grams at hatching, but by the time that the
chick is able to fly away it is between 16
and 26 grams. This is a staggering
increase, given that it takes place over a
period of just three weeks. The amount of
food needed to fuel this growth is equally
staggering: for example, thousands of
caterpillars are delivered to a typical brood
of chicks raised in mature oak woodland.
These dominate the Great Tit chicks’ diet,
contributing some 60 to 95%, depending
on the habitat, but their numbers fluctuate
throughout the year.
Great Tits do better if they can match
the period of peak demand from their
chicks with periods of caterpillar
abundance. However, research shows that
climate change is affecting the Great Tits’
egg-laying pattern. In Britain, for example,
they are laying their eggs an average of
eight days earlier than they did in the
1960s, in response to warmer springs.
Caterpillars are also emerging earlier, but
the date on which they do so is influenced
by temperatures experienced after the
birds have already produced their eggs. By
then there is little that the birds can do to
advance hatching, so chicks may be
produced too late, resulting in their reduced
growth rates and even starvation. If this
imbalance continues, it may have long-
term implications for the Great Tit.
